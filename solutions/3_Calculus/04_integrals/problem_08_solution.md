# Problem 8: Choosing the Factors in Integration by Parts

## Problem Statement

Compute
$$\int x\cos x\,dx.$$
Before calculating, justify why it is convenient to differentiate $x$ and integrate $\cos x$, rather than the other way around.

## Theoretical Background and Concepts

- **Integration by Parts Formula (Reversing the Product Rule)**:
  Derived from the product rule $\frac{d}{dx}[u(x)v(x)] = u'(x)v(x) + u(x)v'(x)$, the integration-by-parts formula states:
  $$\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du.$$
- **Principles for Choosing $u$ and $dv$**:
  To apply integration by parts successfully to $\int f(x)g(x)\,dx$, we must split the integrand $f(x)g(x)\,dx$ into a factor $u$ (to be differentiated) and a differential $dv$ (to be integrated) according to two criteria:
  1. $dv$ must be a differential that we can easily integrate to find $v = \int dv$.
  2. The new integral $\int v\,du$ must be **simpler** (or at least no more complicated) than the original integral $\int u\,dv$.
  For a product of a polynomial $P(x)$ and a trigonometric or exponential function ($\cos x$, $\sin x$, $e^x$), we choose $u = P(x)$ because differentiation lowers the degree of a polynomial, whereas integrating a polynomial raises its degree.
- **Summary of Core Integration Concepts**: Alongside integration by parts, basic integration relies on the power rule ($\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C$), the constant of integration $C \in \mathbb{R}$ representing vertical shifts, $u$-substitution (reversing the chain rule), and the Newton-Leibniz formula ($\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a)$) for definite integrals, which measures net signed area rather than unsigned geometric area.

## Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

### 1. Recognizing the Method and Justifying the Choice of Factors
The integrand $x\cos x$ is a product of a linear polynomial $x$ and a trigonometric function $\cos x$. Since neither factor is the derivative of an inner composite argument of the other, we use **integration by parts**.

We have two possible ways to assign $u$ and $dv$. Let us compare them before calculating:

- **Choice A (Differentiate $x$, integrate $\cos x$) — Convenient Choice**:
  Set $u = x$ and $dv = \cos x\,dx$.
  - Differentiating $u = x$ gives $du = 1\,dx$, which lowers the degree of the polynomial from $1$ to $0$ and **eliminates the factor $x$ completely**.
  - Integrating $dv = \cos x\,dx$ gives $v = \sin x$, which remains a simple elementary trigonometric function of the same complexity.
  - Consequently, the new integral $\int v\,du = \int \sin x\,dx$ is an immediate elementary integral.

- **Choice B (Differentiate $\cos x$, integrate $x$) — Counterproductive Choice**:
  Suppose we instead set $u = \cos x$ and $dv = x\,dx$.
  - Differentiating $u = \cos x$ gives $du = -\sin x\,dx$ (still trigonometric).
  - Integrating $dv = x\,dx$ gives $v = \frac{1}{2}x^2$, which **increases the degree of the polynomial** from $1$ to $2$.
  - Applying $\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du$ would give:
    $$\int x\cos x\,dx = \frac{1}{2}x^2\cos x + \frac{1}{2}\int x^2\sin x\,dx.$$
    The new integral $\int x^2\sin x\,dx$ has a higher power of $x$ and is **more complicated** than the original integral $\int x\cos x\,dx$.

Therefore, we must choose $u = x$ and $dv = \cos x\,dx$.

### 2. Setting Up $u, dv, du, v$
We write out the four components explicitly:
$$\begin{aligned}
u &= x &\implies \quad du &= 1\,dx = dx, \\
dv &= \cos x\,dx &\implies \quad v &= \int \cos x\,dx = \sin x.
\end{aligned}$$

### 3. Computing the Integral via Integration by Parts
Substituting $u$, $v$, and $du$ into the formula $\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du$:
$$\begin{aligned}
\int x\cos x\,dx &= x\sin x - \int \sin x\,dx.
\end{aligned}$$
Now we evaluate the remaining basic integral $\int \sin x\,dx = -\cos x + C$:
$$\begin{aligned}
\int x\cos x\,dx &= x\sin x - (-\cos x) + C \\
&= x\sin x + \cos x + C, \quad C \in \mathbb{R}.
\end{aligned}$$

## Verification and Consistency Checks

We verify our result $F(x) = x\sin x + \cos x + C$ by differentiating it step-by-step with respect to $x$, using the **Product Rule** on $x\sin x$:

$$\begin{aligned}
F'(x) &= \frac{d}{dx}\bigl(x\sin x + \cos x + C\bigr) \\
&= \frac{d}{dx}(x\sin x) + \frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) + \frac{d}{dx}(C) \\
&= \left[\left(\frac{d}{dx}x\right)\sin x + x\left(\frac{d}{dx}\sin x\right)\right] + (-\sin x) + 0 \quad \text{(by the Product Rule)} \\
&= 1 \cdot \sin x + x\cos x - \sin x \\
&= x\cos x.
\end{aligned}$$

The extra term $+\sin x$ generated by differentiating $x\sin x$ cancels with $-\sin x$ from differentiating $\cos x$, recovering the exact original integrand $x\cos x$.
